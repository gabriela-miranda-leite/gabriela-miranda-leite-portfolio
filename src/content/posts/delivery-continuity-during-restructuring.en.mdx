# Ensuring delivery continuity during team restructuring

Throughout every developer's career, changes in tech teams are a constant. Whether driven by product restructuring, shifting business priorities, or leadership transitions, instability is a common scenario. The real challenge is that teams are often not structured to absorb these impacts immediately.

When a change directly affects the management or leadership of a squad, the cascade effect is almost instant: uncertainty about the future sets in, doubts arise about what's truly a priority, how the backlog will be managed, and how rituals will work. Without clear direction, anxiety takes over and the delivery flow starts to stall.

As a developer who aspires to move into engineering management, this experience completely changed my perspective. It became much clearer how those invisible efforts managers put into the *board* are fundamental to bringing psychological safety to the team and predictability to the business.

The first turning point in my view was about *tasks* arriving on the *board* without clear criteria or *story point* estimates. This is a classic "love-hate" topic between developers and managers. For those who are coding, the scoring and bureaucracy of refinement often feels like nothing more than an official guess or a waste of time. But when you look at it through the lens of management, you understand that the problem isn't the number itself — it's the void of context. A task that comes in raw is an invitation to rework and frustration.

This systemic view of the *board*, however, came with practice. Throughout my career, observing and absorbing the best facilitation dynamics, I realized the real importance that this number has for the team as a whole.

For the Product Manager, this number is the tool to understand the viable delivery volume and commit to realistic goals for the company. For the Tech Manager, it serves to track delivery metrics, avoid team overload, and ensure predictability. For developers, it helps to size the effort of each task.

Therefore, understanding a team's *capacity* is vital, as is seeking standardization in how scoring is done within the tribe or company to maintain fair criteria. In practice, if we think of a *sprint* of 10 business days (excluding weekends and planned holidays), the ideal average delivery per developer tends to hover around 13 points. Of course, this number varies — whether due to estimation biases or unforeseen events — but over time the trend stabilizes.

To illustrate how we usually structure this effort scale and guide refinement, I use a practical visual reference:

![Story points scale showing the scoring range and when to break down stories](/posts/delivery-continuity-during-restructuring/story-points-scale.png)

As the diagram shows, the goal of continuous refinement is precisely to break down larger demands before they enter the *sprint*, turning what would be a 40-point monster into agile and manageable *stories*.

So, whenever I saw tasks entering *plannings* without estimates, I made a point of raising the flag and explaining the importance of this for the team's health.

> I'll be honest: at first, taking on this role feels like being "the annoying person in the meeting." Changing a team's perception of rituals and process doesn't happen overnight, and it's naive to think everyone will instantly love refinement.
>
> However, the role of leadership — with or without the formal title — is often to sustain that consistency. Over time, the stance of "hammering home" the importance of refinement stops being seen as nagging and starts being recognized for its practical results: fewer last-minute surprises, clearer tasks, and a much more predictable workflow for everyone.

Finally, even in difficult times, the team needs to keep going: the rituals, the deliveries. As someone who deeply values organization, I saw that one of the most important rituals is refinement. It's the moment to understand whether everyone comprehends what needs to be done, whether the *task* is clear, whether there are internal or external blockers, whether it's feasible, whether there are suggestions, among many other points — and, of course, to estimate the *task*.

So, it was a ritual I wanted to lead from the front: schedule it on the calendar and create the space to analyze what we still had pending in that *sprint*, knowing how much we needed to refine, organize, rewrite, break down, or create new tasks as they emerged. Doing this was essential to understanding more about where our product was heading and to even question things more, with authority. In the end, the stance stops being seen as nagging and starts reflecting what truly matters: clearer tasks, fewer surprises, and a predictable workflow for everyone.
